# Elicitation Techniques

## 1. Purpose and Scope

Use this page when **Safe Elicitation Method** may help. Use it only when all of these are true:
- a suitable person may carry relevant context implicitly;
- a current approved record does not already provide the needed information; and
- a specific, focused question can be asked safely and appropriately.

This page provides the most developed practical elicitation guidance within [[Unstated Context Control]], the current detailed Control. The techniques help surface lived experience, repeated-task learning, local practice, organisational preferences, risk appetite, tacit judgement, relationship context, timing context, and other relevant context that may not be recalled or expressed through a broad question.

Elicitation does not prove completeness or truth. It also does not transfer authority to the person answering.

Use the approved-record route instead if a current approved record already provides the needed context. Keep some context human-only, or safely convert it, rather than entering it into AI.

This page is not a complete organisational-context or specialist-interview framework.

### 2.1 Specific Reference Point Principle

A good elicitation question gives the person one specific reference point to think about. It may be a specific event, person or role, timeframe, task, exception, authority boundary, material detail, or wording choice. Keeping the reference point specific helps the person focus on something concrete instead of searching broadly for anything they may have missed.

Avoid broad questions such as `Is there anything else?` unless they are used as the final fallback.

---

### 2.2 How to Shape a Good Elicitation Question

Use this pattern:

```text
Thinking about [one specific reference point], what happened, changed, stood out, or would matter for [this task, output, audience, decision, or approval]?
```

A strong elicitation question should:

1. point to one specific reference point;
2. ask for a concrete memory, observation, exception, reaction, or consequence;
3. connect the answer to the present task;
4. use neutral wording rather than suggesting the answer;
5. ask one question at a time;
6. allow `nothing relevant` as a valid answer;
7. be used only if the person can answer safely and authoritatively.

Do not use a broad category question if a specific reference point is available. Do not accuse, pressure, force a concern, or treat hesitation as evidence.

---

### 2.3 Safety Gate Before Asking

Before asking, check:

- is the person suitable to answer;
- is the question safe and permitted;
- could the answer reveal sensitive, private, restricted, disputed, or unsafe context;
- should the question be asked through an approved process instead;
- should the context be kept outside the AI interaction;
- could a role, function or generalised description be used instead of a real name;
- does the questioner have trust, authority, competence, relationship access, permission and a safe process.

Prefer a role, function or generalised description over a real name unless a real name is necessary, permitted, current and safe. Do not ask for private history, safeguarding details, counselling information, disciplinary details, unverified allegations or relationship-sensitive information through an ordinary process.

If the answer may involve legal, regulatory, privacy, safeguarding, HR, financial, medical, pastoral, theological, or other specialist boundaries, use only the approved route. If no safe approved route is available, stop and escalate.

## 4. Elicitation Techniques

Use only the technique that fits the current task.

---

### 4.1 Last exception / last bent rule

#### When to use

Use when exceptions, local practice, or informal thresholds may change the answer.

#### Helps surface

**Unwritten Context Gap**, **Authority Boundary Blindness**, **Materiality Judgement Gap** — exceptions, risk appetite, informal thresholds, prior approvals, repeated-task learning.

#### Question

```text
When did we last bend, bypass, adapt, or quietly make an exception to this rule, and for whom or why?
```

#### Why it helps

A concrete exception is easier to remember than a general rule. It helps people surface context they may normally apply without naming it.

---

### 4.2 One person who may react

#### When to use

Use when a real reader or affected group may experience the output differently from the drafter.

#### Helps surface

**Reader Interpretation Gap**, **Recipient Impact Blindness**, **Bicultural Register Gap** — relationship context, lived experience, preferences, impact, tone, timing sensitivity.

#### Question

```text
If safe and permitted, describe one real recipient, role, or audience group who may read this differently, feel blamed, be surprised, or be affected.
```

#### Follow-up question

```text
How would this person or audience understand the wording if they read it today?
```

#### Why it helps

It grounds review in a permitted role or audience group without requiring unsafe naming of a person.

---

### 4.3 This week’s context

#### When to use

Use when the task may be affected by recent events, changing conditions, or timing.

#### Helps surface

**Recipient Impact Blindness**, **Reader Interpretation Gap**, **Anomaly Instinct Gap** — recent incidents, current pressures, temporary sensitivities, organisational mood, timing constraints.

#### Question

```text
What happened in the last few days that someone reading this may currently be thinking about?
```

#### Why it helps

People often remember current conditions only when asked about a specific period.

---

### 4.4 New-person walkthrough

#### When to use

Use when the task has practical steps that experienced people do automatically.

#### Helps surface

**Local Operational Memory Gap**, **Unwritten Context Gap**, **Anomaly Instinct Gap** — workarounds, practical constraints, local habits, operational memory, tacit judgement.

#### Question

```text
If you were training a new person to do this task, what would you warn them about first?
```

#### Why it helps

Training a new person reveals warnings and habits that experienced people no longer write down.

---

### 4.5 What went wrong last time

#### When to use

Use when the task resembles a previous mistake, near miss, complaint, or workaround.

#### Helps surface

**Local Operational Memory Gap**, **Anomaly Instinct Gap**, **Materiality Judgement Gap** — failure patterns, exception handling, hidden constraints, supplier habits, risky assumptions.

#### Question

```text
What went wrong, nearly went wrong, or had to be worked around last time?
```

#### Why it helps

A recent problem gives memory a concrete anchor and can reveal what the AI task basis omits.

---

### 4.6 Authority boundary

#### When to use

Use when the output may approve, promise, decide, authorise, refuse, or speak for someone.

#### Helps surface

**Authority Boundary Blindness**, **Unwritten Context Gap** — authority limits, delegation, actual approval practice, decision ownership.

#### Question

```text
What decision does this output appear to make, and who actually has authority to make it?
```

#### Why it helps

It separates drafting from decision-making and prevents AI from crossing an authority boundary.

---

### 4.7 Materiality check

#### When to use

Use when a detail may change a decision, approval, funding, trust, or next action.

#### Helps surface

**Materiality Judgement Gap**, **Authority Boundary Blindness**, **Unwritten Context Gap** — material thresholds, risk appetite, preferences, board or funder expectations, consequences.

#### Question

```text
What detail, if wrong or missing, would change the decision, approval, funding, trust, or next action?
```

#### Why it helps

It focuses on what matters enough to change the outcome, not on every possible detail.

---

### 4.8 Bilingual / register check

#### When to use

Use when wording crosses language, culture, register, or audience expectations.

#### Helps surface

**Bicultural Register Gap**, **Reader Interpretation Gap**, **Recipient Impact Blindness** — tone, politeness, formality, authority signal, local terms, bicultural meaning.

#### Question

```text
Does this wording carry the same meaning, tone, politeness, formality, and authority signal for the real audience?
```

#### Why it helps

Fluent wording can still carry the wrong social signal. This question checks meaning and relationship fit.

---

### 4.9 Final fallback

#### When to use

Use when the reviewer cannot name the missing context but senses that the task basis may still be thin.

#### Helps surface

**AI Blind Spot**, **Unwritten Context Gap**, **Anomaly Instinct Gap** — unknown gaps, tacit judgement, overlooked context, residual uncertainty.

#### Question

```text
What am I not thinking to mention?
```

#### Why it helps

It gives the reviewer one final neutral opening without pressuring them to invent details.

If it is safe to do so, briefly explain the purpose or consequence of the review. Then split the broad fallback into a few small checks, such as:
- a number that may be outdated;
- a name attached to a commitment; or
- wording that may be more certain or official than intended.

Do not use stakes framing if it may increase the cost of speaking up, imply blame, or pressure the reviewer to produce a finding.

If the reviewer answers `nothing`, use a neutral follow-up to identify what they checked before reaching that answer. If the material was only skimmed, treat the answer as not fully checked.

A pause, uncertainty, hesitation, vague answer, change of subject, or confident tone is not evidence of a fact. Do not record it as proof. Use a neutral follow-up only if it is safe. Otherwise use the Reviewer Standing Check, an approved alternative reviewer, or the Stop Rule.

Do not require a finding. Do not say that multiple reviewers prove completeness. Do not make reading aloud mandatory.

## 5. Stop Rules

Stop rules identify when elicitation should pause or move to an approved process or reviewer with suitable standing. They sit outside the elicitation-technique count.

---

### 5.1 When to Stop Asking Questions

#### When to use

Use this when the issue is sensitive, political, deliberately unspoken, outside the reviewer’s authority, or known only to someone with standing.

#### Helps surface

Situations in which elicitation is not enough and reviewer standing, process safety, or escalation is required.

#### Question

```text
Should we stop asking this here and use an approved process or reviewer with standing instead?
```

#### Why it helps

Better questions are not enough when asking would be unsafe, unauthorised, or outside the questioner’s role. In that case, use [[When Questions Are Not Enough]] and [[Templates/Reviewer Standing Check]].

The Stop Rule is not a tenth elicitation technique.
